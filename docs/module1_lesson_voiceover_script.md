# Module 1 — Lessons 1–6 recording sheet

Prepared from the current lesson definitions, examples, clues and simulation briefs on 16 November 2026. These are **lesson** recordings, separate from the story chapter recordings.

## Voice and delivery

Use the Handler's existing voice: clear, interested, confident and supportive, like a capable teammate. Aim for 135–150 words per minute. Keep the explanation conversational; use a slightly more focused, mission-control delivery for the briefing. Avoid a robotic filter, shouting, or artificial urgency. Leave about half a second before and after each recording; do not add background music.

Record each filename as a separate clip. Speak only the blockquoted script, not filenames or direction notes. Natural punctuation pauses are welcome; scene timing will follow your delivery. The subtitles will be matched to the final recordings when they are supplied.

Keep the seven existing **M01_L01_VO_01–07** video recordings. They already belong to Lesson 1's walkthrough and do not need to be recorded again.

Pronunciation: read @ as “at,” . as “dot,” IT as “I T,” PDF as “P D F,” and exe as “E X E.” In **harb0r**, explicitly say “harbor with a zero instead of the letter O.” In **westfie1d**, say “Westfield with the number one instead of the letter L.” Read ₱350 as “three hundred fifty pesos.” These fictional addresses are training examples; you do not need to spell every ordinary character. Read headings such as From and Subject naturally as part of the example.

The quiz uses a rotating question bank. These recordings teach the lesson; they do not narrate randomized quiz questions or reveal answers during a graded attempt.

## Lesson 1 — The fake school email

### M01_L01_01_Definition.mp3

**Delivery:** Warm and confident. Emphasize the safe action, not fear.

> A fake email that pretends to be a place you already trust, hoping you will click and hand over a secret.

> Check the full sender address and destination. Verify through a known site; never reply with a password or one-time code.

### M01_L01_02_Context.mp3

**Delivery:** Read the email in a slightly official voice. Let “twelve minutes” sound pressuring, without shouting.

> From: Harbor Civic IT <help@harb0r-civic.net>
> Subject: Campus login expires in 12 minutes

> Your campus password stops working at 9:15. Open this page now and type it again so you are not locked out of class: harb0r-civic.net/login-fix

### M01_L01_03_Clues.mp3

**Delivery:** Observant, explaining what the learner can actually see. Pause between clues.

> The zero in harb0r imitates the school name. A familiar display name is not proof.

> They want your campus password entered on their page.

> The countdown is meant to rush you past verification.

## Lesson 2 — The name on the left can lie

### M01_L02_01_Definition.mp3

### M01_L02_03_Clues.mp3

**Delivery:** Observant, explaining what the learner can actually see. Pause between clues.

### M01_L02_04_Safe_Response.mp3

**Delivery:** Clear and reassuring. Treat this as the practical takeaway, not a victory speech.

> Safety net: never send a password by email. Open the real portal yourself if a ticket matters.

> Check the full sender address and destination. Verify through a known site; never reply with a password or one-time code.

### M01_L02_05_Mission_Brief.mp3

**Delivery:** Focused mission-control delivery. A short pause after the operation title, then curiosity in the hook and clear instructions.

> Operation: Name Tag.

> The help desk says your ticket is back. Time to check who is actually behind that name tag.

> Read the ticket email in Mail and inspect its details. Ask the trusted Directory contact to verify the request, then choose your response.

> Anyone can type an official-looking name. Independent checks protect your password, even when a message sounds helpful.
